Patient Guide

How international patients access U.S. clinical trials — step by step.

A practical overview of what the path from "I heard the best option might be in the U.S." to actually being evaluated by a U.S. center really looks like, and where it commonly gets stuck.

Step 1 — Get a clear picture of your diagnosis

The single biggest factor in whether a U.S. trial search succeeds is how specific the starting diagnosis is. A confirmed genetic subtype, staging information, or detailed specialist notes narrow the search dramatically compared to a general condition name. If your diagnosis is still unclear, that's not a dead end — it just changes what you're looking for, from a specific trial to a specialized diagnostic program.

Step 2 — Search or request a search of active U.S. trials

Public registries such as ClinicalTrials.gov list trials that are actively enrolling and are open to anyone to search directly. The practical difficulty most international patients and families run into isn't finding listings — it's determining which listings are a genuine match for their specific case, whether the trial is actually still enrolling, and whether the site accepts international patients at all. This is where an independent case review, rather than a keyword search, tends to save the most time.

Step 3 — Verify eligibility before getting your hopes up

Trial eligibility criteria are specific and unforgiving — age ranges, prior treatment history, exact genetic markers, and disease stage can all disqualify an otherwise promising-looking match. Verifying eligibility against your actual records, before you invest emotional and financial resources in pursuing an option, is worth doing carefully rather than quickly.

Step 4 — Organize records into a submittable application

U.S. institutions typically require records in a specific format and, often, in English. Assembling a complete, well-organized application — rather than a folder of scattered documents — materially affects both the speed and quality of the response you get back.

Step 5 — Coordinate evaluation, travel, and the hospital's intake process

If accepted, there's a second logistics layer: scheduling evaluations, coordinating around the receiving institution's calendar, arranging accommodation, and navigating medical visa documentation. None of this is clinical, but all of it affects whether the opportunity a patient qualifies for is actually usable in practice.

Step 6 — Stay in contact through treatment

For longer courses of treatment, having a single consistent point of contact between the patient's family and the U.S. medical team — rather than starting over with communication each time — tends to reduce friction meaningfully over time.
